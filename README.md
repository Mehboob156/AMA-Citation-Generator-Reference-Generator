## What Is AMA Citation Style?

AMA citation style is a referencing system used primarily in medical, health-science, and scientific publications. It uses numbered references and superscript numerals within the text to connect statements with their corresponding sources.

Unlike styles that arrange references alphabetically, AMA generally numbers sources in the order they first appear in the manuscript. Each source retains its assigned number whenever it is cited again.

A properly formatted journal reference typically includes the authors, article title, abbreviated journal name, publication year, volume, issue, page range, and DOI when available.

Understanding these requirements helps researchers maintain consistent citations throughout a paper.

## Understanding AMA In-Text Citations

AMA uses superscript Arabic numerals to identify sources cited in the body of a paper. These numbers correspond to the numbered references in the reference list.

For example:

**Example sentence:** Minimally invasive surgical techniques may offer benefits for selected patients.¹

The superscript number points readers to the matching reference at the end of the document. The same source retains its original number when cited again.

Writers should also pay attention to the placement of superscript numerals and follow the specific requirements of their journal, institution, or publisher.

## How to Cite Different Types of Sources in AMA Style

Different sources require different bibliographic details. Identifying the source type before formatting a reference helps reduce errors.

### Journal Articles

Journal references generally include the authors, article title, NLM journal abbreviation, publication year, volume, issue, page range, and DOI when available.

Medical researchers should pay particular attention to journal abbreviations, since AMA references typically use standardized abbreviated titles rather than the full journal name.

### PubMed Articles

PubMed records are especially useful for medical and biomedical research. A PMID is a unique identifier assigned to a PubMed citation, while a DOI identifies a digital publication object. A PMCID identifies a record in PubMed Central.

These identifiers serve different purposes and should not be treated as interchangeable. A PubMed record may also contain journal information, authors, publication dates, and links that help researchers verify a reference.

### Websites

Website references may require a corporate or individual author, webpage title, website name, publication or update date, access date when applicable, and URL. The required elements depend on the type of webpage and the applicable AMA guidance.

Always check the actual page rather than relying exclusively on automatically retrieved metadata.

### Books and Other Academic Sources

Book references may require the author or editor, book title, edition, publisher, publication year, and relevant page range. Chapter references may need additional information about the chapter authors and the book in which the chapter appears.

Checking the source category before generating the citation helps ensure the reference includes the appropriate details.

## Common AMA Referencing Mistakes to Avoid

Even when using a citation generator, researchers should watch for several frequent problems:

- **Incorrect author information:** Missing authors, misspelled names, or an incorrect author order can affect the accuracy of a reference.
- **Wrong journal abbreviations:** A journal's full title may not be the required form for an AMA reference.
- **Missing publication details:** Volume, issue, page numbers, dates, or DOI information may be incomplete.
- **Incorrect numbering:** References should follow the order in which sources first appear in the manuscript.
- **Unverified metadata:** A generated citation may look correct while containing inaccurate source details.
- **Inconsistent publisher requirements:** A journal or university may provide additional instructions that need to be followed.

Using a generator for formatting and then checking the result against the original source offers a more reliable workflow than copying citations without review.
